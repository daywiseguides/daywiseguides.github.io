Starting Social Security Retirement Benefits Early: What SSA Says

When should you start Social Security retirement benefits? The Social Security Administration (SSA) says you can begin as early as age 62, but you are entitled to your full benefit only at your full retirement age. This page summarizes SSA's Benefits Planner page on starting early.

Early, full and delayed

  • Starting at 62 means a smaller monthly amount, reduced for each month before full retirement age.
  • Starting at full retirement age gives the full benefit.
  • Waiting past full retirement age, up to age 70, increases the monthly amount through delayed retirement credits.

Full retirement age depends on birth year

SSA's chart lists it as 66 for people born from 1943 to 1954, rising by two months a year to 66 and 10 months for 1959, and 67 for 1960 and later.

What the reduction looks like

SSA's example uses a $1,000 benefit at full retirement age. For someone born in 1960 or later, claiming at 62 would cut that to $700, a 30% reduction. For someone born in 1955, it would be $741, about 25.83% less. SSA notes the percentages are approximate and that a spouse's benefit is reduced by a larger share.

Things SSA says to keep in mind

  • Collecting early means more months of payments but a lower amount each month.
  • Each person's situation is different, so weigh your own planning factors.
  • Even if you delay benefits, SSA says to apply for Medicare within 3 months of your 65th birthday, or Part B and Part D may cost more later.
  • If you qualify as a survivor, your full retirement age may differ.
