Mortgage Escrow Accounts: What They Are and Why Lenders Use Them (CFPB)

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that an escrow account, sometimes called an impound account depending on where you live, is set up by your mortgage lender to pay certain property-related expenses. The page shows no update date, so we checked it on November 26, 2026.

How it works

A portion of each monthly mortgage payment goes into the account. Your servicer then pays property taxes and insurance bills for you, so you avoid one or two big bills a year. Many lenders require escrow for taxes and insurance, and sometimes the law requires it.

Payments can change

Tax and insurance costs can change from year to year, so your escrow payment, and your total monthly payment, can change too.

If your loan has no escrow

You must plan and budget for those large bills yourself. The CFPB says that if you do not pay property taxes, your state or local government may add fines and penalties or place a tax lien on your home, and you could face foreclosure. If you fail to pay taxes or insurance, your lender may add the amounts to your loan balance, add an escrow account to your loan, or buy insurance for you and bill you. This "force-placed" insurance is usually more expensive than a policy you buy and generally protects only the lender's interest.

Asking for one

Even when it is not required, the CFPB says to consider asking for an escrow account, since small payments are easier to budget than a large annual bill.
