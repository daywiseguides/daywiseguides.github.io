How to Build an Emergency Fund: What the CFPB Says

An emergency fund is cash set aside for surprise costs, such as a car repair, a medical bill, or a drop in income. The CFPB says it helps you recover faster from a shock and stay on track toward bigger savings goals.

Why it matters

The CFPB notes that people with little savings may lean on credit cards or loans after a surprise, which can turn into debt that is harder to pay off. Some also pull money from retirement savings.

How much?

The CFPB does not give one number. It suggests looking at the surprise expenses you have had before and what they cost, then setting a goal from that. Even a small amount gives some security.

Ways to start

  • Set a specific goal and a regular amount, and check your progress.
  • Look at when money comes in and goes out. You may be able to ask a landlord, utility, or card company to move a due date.
  • Put part of a tax refund or a cash gift into savings.
  • Set up automatic transfers from checking to savings. Watch your balance so you do not trigger overdraft fees.
  • If you are paid by direct deposit, ask your employer whether the paycheck can be split between two accounts.

Where to keep it

The CFPB lists a bank or credit union account (generally considered one of the safest places), a prepaid card, or cash. It warns that cash can be stolen, lost, or destroyed.

When to use it

Decide ahead of time what counts as an emergency. If you use it, rebuild it. The CFPB points out that paying a surprise bill with a card or loan can make it much bigger once interest and fees are added.
